\begin{abstract}
Comparing human-operated and autonomous robots is difficult when their control methods differ. In
student robotics competitions, for example, match scores do not show whether a programmed routine
performs better than a human driver under the same conditions. This paper introduces \mb{}, an open
framework for evaluating robot task performance from externally observed trajectories and task-state
measurements, without access to the agent's controller. \mb{} defines task-specific goals, reports
the share of the goal an attempt achieved beside the level reached by chance, and specifies a
protocol for comparing agents over repeated trials and previously unseen layouts. The framework is
implemented as a deterministic scorer, trajectory-capture tools and a simulator that runs VEX AIM
Python routines unchanged. In a preliminary simulated study of an object-sorting task, a routine that
encodes the public layout scores 4.00 of 6 points there but 2.80 on unseen layouts, while a
camera-based routine scores 2.45 and 2.35. These results illustrate the method; they do not establish
real-world measurement accuracy or any comparison with real people, which remain to be tested. The
implementation and protocol are released so that the method can be examined and validated on
physical robots.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

In VEX robotics competitions, my team spends a lot of time improving autonomous routines. But I kept
coming back to a basic question: was our code actually better than driving the robot ourselves? We
could compare match scores, but those depended on the field, the opposing alliance and everything
else that happened in the match. They did not tell us how much of a task each approach could
complete under the same conditions.

I ran into the same problem working with friends on RoboBench, a project in which large language
models write VEX robot code, the code runs on a robot, and the results are fed back to improve the
next attempt. We needed a consistent way to say whether one attempt was better than another, without
depending on how the code had been written.

\mb{} grew out of that problem. Instead of evaluating the controller, it measures the observable
outcome of a task: where the robot went and what state it left the task in. The same scoring
procedure can then be applied to a human driver, a programmed routine, AI-generated code or a
commercial robot, provided the task and operating conditions are comparable. The research question
is whether such \emph{agent-independent} evaluation can be made fair, repeatable and cheap enough to
run with a phone camera.

This is a framework and methodology paper. Its experiments use simulation, synthetic video, one real
capture and one open dataset; they show how the method works and what it is designed to catch, and
no empirical claim about real robots, real code or real people is made from them. The contributions
are:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item a \textbf{goal-referenced scoring model} that reports the share of a task's goal achieved,
    beside chance, with comparisons to people only where people use the same means
    (Section~\ref{sec:framework});
  \item an \textbf{open implementation} whose only interface to an agent is a recorded trace of
    poses and task state (Section~\ref{sec:impl});
  \item an \textbf{evaluation protocol} with public and unseen layouts, paired comparisons and explicit
    outcomes that separate ``no difference found'' from ``equivalent'' (Section~\ref{sec:protocol});
  \item a \textbf{preliminary simulated study} that runs the protocol end to end
    (Section~\ref{sec:eval}).
\end{enumerate}

what has been demonstrated (in simulation or on synthetic data) and what is not yet shown.

\section{Related work}\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Benchmarks for robot policies.} Meta-World~\cite{metaworld2019}, CALVIN~\cite{calvin2022}
and LIBERO~\cite{libero2023} evaluate learned manipulation policies in simulation;
Habitat~\cite{habitat2019} does so for embodied navigation; SimplerEnv~\cite{simplerenv2024} studies
how well simulated evaluation predicts real-robot results. RoboDojo~\cite{robodojo2026} combines
simulated and real tasks on standardized bimanual hardware, scores real trials by blinded human
evaluators with partial credit, and verifies submissions on hidden layouts.
RoboArena~\cite{roboarena2025} distributes real-world evaluation across institutions through pairwise
comparisons. Evaluating task completion, standardizing tasks and using held-out layouts are not new;
\mb{} adopts explicit protocols, hidden layouts, repeatability reporting and a separation between
verified and unverified results from this literature. What it changes is the unit being evaluated:
the whole agent (robot, software and any human operator), observed from outside.
Table~\ref{tab:compare} summarizes the difference without claiming that other benchmarks could not be
extended in the same direction.

\paragraph{Human reference points.} Human-normalized scores were popularized by the Arcade Learning
Environment~\cite{ale2013,mnih2015}. Section~\ref{sec:framework} explains why \mb{} moved from
normalizing to people to a goal-referenced score.

\paragraph{Reporting under uncertainty.} Agarwal et al.~\cite{agarwal2021} show that point estimates
over few runs mislead and recommend interval estimates. \mb{} uses bootstrap
intervals~\cite{efron1979}, paired designs and an equivalence margin in the spirit of two one-sided
tests~\cite{lakens2017}.

\paragraph{Product test methods.} IEC~62929~\cite{iec62929} specifies laboratory methods for household
cleaning robots. \mb{} is complementary: it aims to measure robots where they are used, from
ordinary video.

\section{The \mb{} framework}\label{sec:framework}

\paragraph{What is measured.} \mb{} scores task outcomes from externally observable trajectories and
task-state measurements, without access to the agent's controller. A trace holds timestamped poses
$(t,x,y,\theta)$ of the robot, optional events (for example ``target knocked down'') and, for tasks
that move objects, the final positions of those objects. The scorer combines these with the task
specification (arena geometry, robot footprint, goal, budget). For waypoint and coverage tasks the
goal depends on the robot's path alone; for delivery tasks it depends on where the objects ended up,
which the robot's path cannot determine and must be measured separately.

\paragraph{Tasks.} A task specifies an arena (size, obstacles, zones), the robot footprint, a time
budget, public layouts, rules for generating unseen layouts, and a goal of one of three kinds:
\emph{waypoints} (reach each in order within position and heading tolerances), \emph{delivery} (each
object rests in its target zone at the end) or \emph{coverage} (share of free floor swept by a tool of
given width). Table~\ref{tab:tasks} lists the current task specifications and which of them are used
in this paper; they use competition hardware but never copy a season's game. A task carries its own
version (after the @); the trace format and scoring rules are versioned by the specification (0.2),
and the software by the scorer version (0.1.0). Every report records all three.

\paragraph{Primary outcome: goal score.} For a run with success $S$ in task units and maximum
$S_{\max}$, the goal score is $g = S/S_{\max}\in[0,1]$. For each agent the board reports the mean over
its runs with a 95\% bootstrap interval, the worst run, and the \emph{completion rate} (share of runs
that achieved the whole goal). Time to goal is reported over runs that achieved it. The goal score is
the headline and the only quantity used in this paper's comparisons.

\paragraph{Chance.} Each task reports the goal score of a randomly moving robot beside the agents'
scores, without subtracting it. On coverage, a random walk already covers about half a room; readers
should see that rather than have it absorbed into a transformation.

\paragraph{Comparison with people (optional).} The first specification normalized every agent to the
median person on the task, following~\cite{mnih2015}. That turned differences in equipment into
apparent differences in skill when means differed (a robot vacuum against a person with a stick
vacuum), left tasks without a human baseline unscored, and saturated where most people finish.
Specification 0.2 reports
$\mathrm{vs\_people}=(S-S_{\text{chance}})/(S_{\text{people}}-S_{\text{chance}})$ only on tasks
declared people-comparable, only when people's median beats chance by at least 10\% of $S_{\max}$
(otherwise the ratio is unstable), and never as the headline.

\paragraph{Secondary measures.} Boards also report path efficiency, consistency and safety, defined
precisely in Appendix~\ref{app:index}. Path efficiency compares the path driven with a simple
reference length computed by the scorer (straight lines through the waypoints reached; a greedy
nearest-first single-carry tour for deliveries; covered area divided by tool width for coverage). The
reference ignores obstacles and robot kinematics, so it is a yardstick, not a shortest path.
An optional index combines these measures with the goal score for ranking within one task. Combining
them is a value judgement with known problems: consistency rewards an agent that always scores 1 of 6
over a better agent with varied results, and one safety violation means different things on
different tasks. The index is therefore secondary, never averaged across tasks, not used in this
paper's conclusions, and can be ignored; its definition and weight sensitivity are in
Appendix~\ref{app:index}.

\paragraph{Ranks.} For display, agents share a rank while their intervals overlap the leader's. This
is a presentation convention, not a test of equivalence.

\section{Implementation}\label{sec:impl}

\paragraph{Traces and the scorer.} A run is a directory with a manifest (task, layout, agent and its
class, capture method, tier, file hashes), timestamped poses, optional events and final object
positions. This is the only interface between an agent and the benchmark. The scorer is implemented
once in Rust and called from Python and the command line; it validates traces (hashes, monotonic time,
sampling gaps), computes run reports and builds boards. A separate recount, written in JavaScript in
the toolkit's report page, recomputes waypoints, deliveries and coverage from raw poses and flags
disagreements with the scorer. Both were written with the same AI assistant, so they are not fully
independent checks. Returning the same report for the same trace says nothing about whether the
trace or the task specification is right; capture accuracy and task design are examined separately.

\paragraph{Capture.} With printed AprilTags~\cite{olson2011} in the arena corners and on the robot, a
per-frame homography maps the image to the floor. Without tags, frames of a fixed or hand-held camera
are aligned to the first frame; the per-pixel median gives an empty-floor image and the robot is the
region that differs from it. The floor plane comes from four image points outlining a floor
rectangle~\cite{hartley2004}; focal length and scale come from a measured width and length, or from
the robot's known size. The robot's position is taken half a robot behind the lowest point of its
silhouette, and its heading from its direction of travel, so tag-free capture cannot measure heading
while the robot turns in place. Floor the camera cannot see is excluded from coverage and safety.

\paragraph{Simulation.} A \emph{game} is defined once (field, zones, wall-mounted fiducials, objects,
layouts, layout rules, match length, scoring) and then simulated many times; a game may link to a task
with the same layouts, in which case simulated runs are also scored by the standard scorer. The first
platform is VEX AIM, a small holonomic classroom robot with a magnetic pick-up, a kicker and a camera.
The simulator runs VEXcode AIM Python files unmodified: the robot API is replaced by stand-ins, every
call advances simulated time, speeds are capped at the published API limits (200\,mm/s,
150\textdegree/s), and each run draws its own wheel slip, drift, turn overshoot, kick scatter and
camera noise. These are modelling assumptions; speed limits alone do not validate the simulator's
dynamics, pick-up, kicking, perception or collisions against a real AIM robot. The
\emph{parameterized simulated-driver baseline} is a set of controller policies with explicit traits
(reaction time with partial anticipation, share of full speed used, steering shake, placement
precision, choice of next object, mistake and hesitation rates). Six named characters span a range of
skill, and the \emph{typical simulated driver} is the median member of a simulated class of twenty.
The traits were chosen by hand to be plausible and are listed in Appendix~\ref{app:drivers}; they are
not fitted to real students.

\section{Evaluation protocol}\label{sec:protocol}

\paragraph{Tiers.} Results are \emph{simulated} (S), \emph{field} (F: a real robot captured and
submitted by its owner, reviewed before publication) or \emph{verified} (V). They are never mixed
without labels. The verified tier is a proposed procedure that has not yet been carried out: it
requires a layout sent at the start, one uncut fixed-camera video with a code word, every attempt
reported, and a second reviewer re-scoring at least one run from raw video; reviewers declare
conflicts of interest (details in the protocol document in the repository).

\paragraph{Runs and layouts.} An agent needs at least five runs on a task to be ranked (ten
recommended); runs are consecutive attempts and every attempt is reported. Public layouts are
published; unseen layouts are generated from each task's layout rules with an unpublished seed.

\paragraph{Paired comparisons.} In simulation, run $i$ of every agent uses the same seed, so the same
layout and the same draw of robot imperfections, and a comparison is the mean of paired differences.
Intervals are 95\% percentile bootstrap intervals from 4{,}000 resamples. On one layout the resampling
unit is the paired run; across unseen layouts the bootstrap is hierarchical (layouts, then runs within
each chosen layout). Paired simulated runs share the simulator and its assumptions, so they are not
independent experimental replicates in the sense of separate physical sessions.

\paragraph{Outcomes.} \emph{Better} or \emph{worse} when the interval excludes zero; \emph{equivalent}
when the interval lies strictly inside a margin of $\pm\delta$; \emph{no difference found} otherwise.
Requiring a 95\% interval inside the margin is a conservative version of the two one-sided tests
procedure~\cite{lakens2017}, which uses a 90\% interval. For the sorting task $\delta=0.5$ points, half
of the smallest scoring unit (one barrel), fixed before the study; it is a convention, not derived
from what matters to users, and other tasks will need their own. ``No difference found'' does not mean
equivalent.

\section{Preliminary evaluation}\label{sec:eval}

All numbers in this section describe the simulation or synthetic video. Every agent, the robot model
and every driver trait are assumptions, so they illustrate what the protocol reports and what it
catches; they are not measurements of VEX AIM robots or of students.

\subsection{Does a public-layout advantage generalize?}

\paragraph{Setup.} One VEX AIM robot must sort three orange and three blue barrels into the zone of
their colour on a 1.83\,m\,$\times$\,1.22\,m table within 45\,s (one point per barrel; six maximum).
Four routines, written for the public layout, span common approaches: \emph{dead reckoning} (a fixed
plan, no sensors); \emph{vision} (the camera finds the nearest barrel, wheel odometry returns it to the
zone); \emph{vision + tags} (as vision, then homing on the AprilTag behind each zone); and
\emph{planner} (the barrel positions of the public layout, an efficient order, full speed, the camera
only for the last few centimetres, and spread drop positions). Each routine, each driver character and
the typical simulated driver play 20 paired runs on the public layout, and 20 paired runs over ten
unseen layouts (two per layout; same start, barrels placed at random away from the zones, the start
and each other).

\paragraph{Results.} Table~\ref{tab:verdict} summarizes the outcome. On the public layout the planner
scores 4.00 points, ahead of the typical simulated driver (2.90) by a paired difference of $+1.10$
($+1.00$ to $+1.25$). On unseen layouts it scores 2.80: a change of $-1.20$ ($-1.60$ to $-0.85$) and
no difference found against the typical simulated driver ($-0.05$; $-0.65$ to $+0.60$). The vision
routine scores 2.45 on the public layout and 2.35 on unseen layouts; its change ($-0.10$; $-0.55$ to
$+0.40$) is small, but the interval is wider than the margin, so the outcome is no difference found,
not equivalence. The dead-reckoning routine, which also encodes the public layout, changes by
$-1.05$ ($-1.50$ to $-0.60$). Interval endpoints are exact at the resolution of the data: with 20 runs
and integer points, bootstrap means fall on multiples of 0.05.

\paragraph{What the study shows, and what it does not.} The planner's public-layout advantage comes
largely from knowing where the barrels are. That a routine which memorizes positions does worse when
they move is expected; the point of the study is that the protocol catches it, and that a verdict
based on the public layout alone would have recommended the planner. The within-routine change does
not depend on the simulated drivers. Several limits apply. First, the planner was chosen as the
strongest routine \emph{on the public layout}, so its public-layout margin is inflated by that
selection; the unseen layouts were not used for selection or development and give the less biased
estimate. Second, ten unseen layouts with two runs each is a small sample of layouts, so the
intervals may understate layout-to-layout variability. Layout by layout
(Appendix~\ref{app:layouts}) the planner scores above the typical simulated driver on four layouts,
the same on three and below on three; more layouts are needed before such a comparison could be
relied on. Third, at the
simulated top speed no agent sorts all six barrels in 45\,s (best runs score 4 or 5). The goal score
therefore measures partial progress under a tight budget rather than completion; this is a property
of the task as specified, and whether the budget should be lengthened is an open question for the
task's next version.

\subsection{Demonstrations on real data}

\begin{figure}[tbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[height=7.5cm]{figs/hallway-tracked.jpg}
\caption{Tag-free capture of a household robot vacuum from one hand-held phone video. Scale came
from the robot's size; floor the camera could not see is excluded. There is no ground truth for this

\label{fig:vacuum}

\paragraph{A robot vacuum.} A household robot vacuum was filmed for 137\,s with a hand-held phone,
without tags (Figure~\ref{fig:vacuum}). The pipeline estimated that it covered 85\% of 3.24\,m$^2$ of
visible floor with a 30\,cm cleaning width, driving 18.6\,m. This shows the pipeline running end to
end on real footage; without ground truth it says nothing about accuracy.

\paragraph{People in an open dataset.} I scored 120 motion-capture walks along one route (about 7\,m)
from THÖR-MAGNI~\cite{thormagni2024}. All 120 reached the goal (median 6.8\,s), so the goal score
could not separate walkers; with a 60\,s budget a random walk reached the goal in 40\% of runs. The
example shows that budgets need to follow typical completion times, or the goal score saturates.

\section{Discussion and limitations}\label{sec:limits}

The evidence supports three modest statements: the framework can be specified precisely and
implemented reproducibly; its protocol, applied to simulated agents, distinguishes a public-layout
advantage from one that generalizes; and tag-based and tag-free capture are accurate to a few
centimetres on synthetic video. It does not yet support claims about real robots or people.

The main limitations follow. The simulator is custom and uncalibrated: its robot model, noise and
every driver trait are assumptions, and simulated agents are less variable than real ones (some score
the same in every run), so intervals are likely narrower than they will be on real robots. Tag-free
capture needs a visible floor rectangle, cannot measure heading during turns in place, and has been
tested only on synthetic video; the effect of capture error on scores is untested. Delivery tasks need
object positions, which the current capture tools do not measure. Goal scores depend on how goals and
budgets are set, and the sorting task's budget may be too tight. The synthetic VEX AIM boards
published on the project site under specification 0.2 contain simulated agents faster than the AIM
robot's top speed; they are marked invalid and are not used here. Coverage scores model cleaning
rather than measure it. Tasks and protocol change only through public RFCs so that such issues are
fixed openly.

\section{Future work}\label{sec:future}

The next step is a small real-world accuracy experiment that does not need a motion-capture lab: a
robot driven between measured floor markers, 20--30 trials filmed with a fixed camera, comparing the
position and waypoint completion estimated by \mb{} (with and without tags) against the measured marks,
and checking whether capture error or missing frames change goal scores. Further work, in order:
scoring the same simulated runs from ground truth and from rendered-video capture to test how capture
error propagates to scores and rankings; more unseen layouts, at several difficulty levels, with
layout-level reporting and a comparison of public-layout and unseen-layout rankings; running the
routines of Section~\ref{sec:eval} on a real AIM robot to measure the simulator's prediction error;
and a paired comparison of real student drivers with their own teams' code, which would require
parental consent, a host institution's youth-protection rules and an ethics review.

\section{Conclusion}

\mb{} proposes evaluating any robot agent, whether a person, code or a product, by how much of a
task's goal it achieves, from what can be observed from outside, with an open scorer and an explicit
protocol. The framework and tools are built and released, and a preliminary simulated study shows why
unseen layouts belong in any verdict. The next step is to test the measurement on real robots.
Comments are welcome at \url{https://motionbench.org/paper}.

\section{Secondary measures and the experimental index}\label{app:index}

\paragraph{Definitions.} Path efficiency $e=\min(1, L_{\text{ref}}/L)$, with $L$ the path driven and
$L_{\text{ref}}$ the reference length: for waypoints, the straight-line length from the start through
the waypoints reached; for deliveries, the greedy tour that repeatedly goes to the nearest remaining
object and carries it to its zone centre, truncated at the number delivered; for coverage, the area
covered divided by the tool width. $e=0$ when nothing was achieved. Consistency
$c=\mathrm{clamp}(1-\sigma_S/\mu_S,0,1)$ with $\sigma_S$ the sample standard deviation of success over
runs, $c=0$ when $\mu_S=0$, and $\sigma_S=0$ for a single run (which is why agents with fewer than five
runs are unranked). Safety $s=1/(1+\bar v)\in(0,1]$ with $\bar v$ the mean number of contact or rule
violations per run. The index is
\[\mathrm{MBI}=0.50\,g+0.20\,e+0.15\,c+0.15\,s\;\in[0,1].\]
The weights are a design choice, not an estimate of what performance means.

\paragraph{When not to use it.} The index should not be used across tasks, to compare agents whose
goal scores differ clearly (the goal score already answers the question), or where consistency is
high only because the score is uniformly low, since $c$ does not distinguish the two.

\paragraph{Weight sensitivity.} On the synthetic boards whose simulated agents respect their robots'
limits, the index was recomputed for 5{,}000 weight vectors drawn near the defaults (Dirichlet with
concentration $50\,w$) and uniformly from the simplex (Table~\ref{tab:sens}). Under local changes the
rankings are stable (median Kendall's $\tau\ge0.94$); under arbitrary weights the coverage ranking is
the least stable. Ranking by goal score alone agrees with the index at $\tau=0.81$ to $0.91$. This
shows the ranking is insensitive to small changes in the weights, not that the weights are right.

\section{Extension: multi-robot matches}\label{app:matches}

The toolkit also includes a seeded 2-versus-2 match simulator, a renderer of broadcast-style video
from a camera position measured on a real event broadcast, a per-robot report and a tracking
benchmark. A baseline tracker finds 90--97\% of robot positions with a median error of 7--9\,cm on
three simulated matches but switches identities 25--44 times per match (IDF1~\cite{ristani2016}
48--66\%). It is infrastructure for future work and is not used in the paper's conclusions.

\begin{abstract}
Robot performance is hard to compare across agents, environments and control methods: a
student driving a robot, the team's autonomous code and a commercial product are rarely
measured the same way. We propose \mb{}, a framework that scores any attempt at a task as the
share of the task's stated goal it achieved, computed from the attempt's recorded trajectory
alone, without access to the agent's software. We describe the scoring model, an open
implementation (a deterministic scorer, a trajectory format, phone-video capture tools, and a
simulator that runs unmodified VEX AIM Python code), and an evaluation protocol with public
and unseen layouts. In a preliminary simulated experiment on a classroom sorting task, the
best of four routines on the public layout outscores a parameterized simulated-driver baseline
by 1.10 points out of 6 (paired 95\% interval 1.00 to 1.25), but on ten unseen layouts its
score falls by 1.20 points and it shows no detectable difference from that baseline, while a
camera-based routine keeps its score. On synthetic video, tag-free capture places the robot
within about 3\,cm (median); it has not yet been validated on real footage, and the
simulated drivers are not calibrated against real people. These two gaps, and a paired
comparison of real drivers with their own code, are the next research objectives.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Students in robot competitions open every match with a period in which the robot runs its own
code, followed by a period of human driving, and they argue about which of the two is better.
This work grew out of that argument in the author's own VEX competitions, and out of RoboBench,
an earlier project with friends: a real-time loop in which large language models write VEX robot
code, the code runs, and the results feed back, to see how quickly the models learn to operate
in a real VEX environment. Both raised the same need for a fair, repeatable way to say how well
an attempt went.
The question generalizes: how much of a job does a given agent --- a person, team-written code,
AI-written code, a person with robot assistance, or a commercial robot --- actually get done?
Answering it fairly is difficult. Match scores mix in opponents and luck; product claims are
best cases; and the strongest research benchmarks evaluate learned control policies on
standardized robots and scenes~\cite{robodojo2026,libero2023,calvin2022,metaworld2019}, which
excludes most of the agents above.

\mb{} starts from the other end: \emph{it fixes the task and its goal, and measures whatever
attempts it}. This paper is a framework paper with preliminary feasibility experiments. Its
contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item a \textbf{goal-referenced scoring model} that reports, for every kind of agent, the share
    of the task's goal achieved, beside the level reached by chance, with comparisons to people
    restricted to tasks where people use the same means (Section~\ref{sec:scoring});
  \item an \textbf{open implementation} in which the only interface between an agent and the
    benchmark is its recorded trajectory (Section~\ref{sec:impl});
  \item an \textbf{evaluation protocol} with public and unseen layouts, paired comparisons and
    explicit outcomes for ``no difference found'' versus ``equivalent'' (Section~\ref{sec:protocol});
  \item a \textbf{preliminary experiment} showing that a routine's advantage on a public layout
    can disappear on unseen layouts (Section~\ref{sec:experiment}).
\end{enumerate}

hypothesis.

\section{Related work}

\paragraph{Benchmarks for robot policies.} Meta-World~\cite{metaworld2019}, CALVIN~\cite{calvin2022}
and LIBERO~\cite{libero2023} evaluate learned manipulation policies in simulation;
Habitat~\cite{habitat2019} does so for embodied navigation; SimplerEnv~\cite{simplerenv2024} studies
how well simulated evaluation predicts real-robot results. RoboDojo~\cite{robodojo2026} combines
simulated and real tasks on standardized bimanual hardware, scores real trials by blinded human
evaluators with partial credit, and verifies submissions on hidden layouts.
RoboArena~\cite{roboarena2025} distributes real-world evaluation across institutions through
pairwise comparisons. These benchmarks standardize the robot and setting, evaluate the policy,
and require integration with their software. \mb{} standardizes the task, evaluates the whole
agent (robot, software and any human operator), and uses only the recorded outcome. We adopt from
this literature explicit protocols, hidden layouts, repeatability reporting and a separation
between verified and unverified results.

\paragraph{Human reference points.} Human-normalized scores were popularized by the Arcade Learning
Environment~\cite{ale2013,mnih2015}. Section~\ref{sec:scoring} explains why \mb{} moved from
normalizing to people to a goal-referenced score.

\paragraph{Reporting under uncertainty.} Agarwal et al.~\cite{agarwal2021} show that point estimates
over few runs mislead and recommend interval estimates; we use bootstrap
intervals~\cite{efron1979}, paired designs and an equivalence margin.

\paragraph{Product test methods.} IEC~62929~\cite{iec62929} specifies laboratory methods for
household cleaning robots. \mb{} is complementary: it measures robots where they are used, from
ordinary video.

\section{Scoring model}\label{sec:scoring}

\paragraph{Tasks.} A task specifies an arena (size, obstacles, zones), the robot footprint, a time
budget, public layouts, layout rules for generating unseen ones, and a goal of one of three kinds:
\emph{waypoints} (reach each in order within position and heading tolerances), \emph{delivery}
(each object rests in its target zone at the end) or \emph{coverage} (share of free floor swept
by a tool of given width). Table~\ref{tab:tasks} lists the current tasks; they use competition
hardware but never copy a season's game.

\paragraph{Versions.} Three version numbers appear and are independent. A \emph{task} carries its own
version after the @ in its name; the two VEX V5 tasks are at 1.0 because their field and rules are
settled, the others at 0.1 because they are experimental. The \emph{specification} (0.2)
versions the trace and board formats and the scoring rules. The \emph{scorer} (0.1.0) versions
the software. Every report records all three, and results under an older specification remain
valid under that specification.

\paragraph{Primary outcome: goal score.} For a run with success $S$ in task units and maximum
$S_{\max}$, the goal score is $g = S/S_{\max}\in[0,1]$. For each agent we report the mean over its
runs with a 95\% bootstrap interval, the worst run, and the \emph{completion rate} (share of runs
that achieved the whole goal). Time to goal is reported over runs that achieved it.

\paragraph{Chance.} Each task reports the goal score of a randomly moving robot beside the agents'
scores, without subtracting it. On coverage, a random walk already covers about half a room;
readers should see that rather than have it absorbed into a transformation.

\paragraph{Comparison with people (optional).} Our first specification normalized every agent to the
median person on the task, following~\cite{mnih2015}. That turned differences in equipment into
apparent differences in skill when means differed (a robot vacuum against a person with a stick
vacuum), left tasks without a human baseline unscored, and saturated where most people finish.
Specification 0.2 reports
$\mathrm{vs\_people}=(S-S_{\text{chance}})/(S_{\text{people}}-S_{\text{chance}})$ only on tasks
declared people-comparable, only when people's median beats chance by at least 10\% of
$S_{\max}$ (otherwise the ratio is unstable and is not reported), and never as the headline.

\paragraph{Secondary measures and an experimental index.} Boards also report path efficiency $e$
(shortest sensible path over path driven, clamped to $[0,1]$), consistency
$c=\mathrm{clamp}(1-\sigma_S/\mu_S, 0, 1)$ with $c=0$ when $\mu_S=0$ and $\sigma_S$ the sample
standard deviation of success over runs, and safety $s=1/(1+\bar v)\in(0,1]$ with $\bar v$ the mean
number of contact or rule violations per run. A single index ranks agents within one task:
\[\mathrm{MBI}=0.50\,g+0.20\,e+0.15\,c+0.15\,s\;\in[0,1].\]
The weights are a design choice, not an estimate; we treat the index as experimental and
secondary to $g$, never average it across tasks, and report its sensitivity to the weights
(Section~\ref{sec:feasibility}).

\paragraph{Ranks.} For display, agents share a rank while their intervals overlap the leader's.
This is a presentation convention, not a test of equivalence.

\section{Implementation}\label{sec:impl}

\paragraph{Trajectory traces and the scorer.} A run is a directory with a manifest (task, layout,
agent and its class, capture method, tier, file hashes), timestamped poses $(t,x,y,\theta)$,
optional events and the final positions of objects. This is the only interface between an agent
and the benchmark, so closed products can be evaluated from video alone. The scorer is
implemented once in Rust and called from Python and the command line; it validates traces
(hashes, monotonic time, sampling gaps), computes run reports and builds boards. An independent
browser page recomputes waypoints, deliveries and coverage from raw poses and flags disagreements.
\emph{Determinism is not accuracy}: the scorer returning the same report for the same trace says
nothing about whether the trace, the task specification or the scoring rules are right, which is
why capture accuracy, task design and the protocol are examined separately.

\paragraph{Capture.} With printed AprilTags~\cite{olson2011} in the arena corners and on the robot,
a per-frame homography maps the image to the floor. Without tags, frames of a fixed or hand-held
camera are aligned to the first frame; the per-pixel median gives an empty-floor image and the
robot is the region that differs from it. The floor plane comes from four image points outlining
a floor rectangle~\cite{hartley2004}; focal length and scale come from a measured width and length,
or from the robot's known size by searching for the camera parameters at which its silhouettes
match. The robot's position is taken half a robot behind the lowest point of its silhouette;
floor the camera cannot see is excluded from coverage and safety.

\paragraph{Simulation: games and drivers.} A \emph{game} is defined once (field, zones, wall-mounted
fiducials, objects, layouts, layout rules, match length, scoring) and then simulated many times;
a game may link to a task with the same layouts, in which case simulated runs are also scored by
the standard scorer and the two scores must agree. The first platform is VEX AIM, a small
holonomic classroom robot with a magnetic pick-up, a kicker and a camera. The simulator runs
VEXcode AIM Python files unmodified: the import header receives stand-ins for the robot, every API
call advances simulated time, speeds follow the published API limits (200\,mm/s, 150\textdegree/s),
and each run draws its own wheel slip, drift, turn overshoot, kick scatter and camera noise. The
\emph{parameterized simulated-driver baseline} consists of controller policies with explicit traits
(reaction time with partial anticipation, share of full speed used, steering shake, placement
precision, choice of next object, mistake and hesitation rates); six named characters span a
plausible classroom, and the \emph{typical simulated driver} is the median member of a simulated
class of twenty. These traits are chosen to be plausible; they are not fitted to real students.

\section{Evaluation protocol}\label{sec:protocol}

\paragraph{Tiers.} Results are \emph{simulated} (S), \emph{field} (F: a real robot captured and
submitted by its owner, reviewed before publication) or \emph{verified} (V). They are never mixed
without labels.

\paragraph{Runs and layouts.} An agent needs at least five runs on a task to be ranked (ten
recommended); runs are consecutive attempts and every attempt is reported. Public layouts are
published; unseen layouts are generated from each task's layout rules with an unpublished seed.

\paragraph{Comparisons.} When two agents are compared in simulation, run $i$ of each uses the same
seed (the same layout and the same draw of robot imperfections), and the comparison is the mean of
paired differences with a 95\% bootstrap interval. The resampling unit is the paired run on a single
layout; across unseen layouts the bootstrap is hierarchical (layouts, then runs within each chosen
layout), so intervals reflect variation across layouts. Outcomes are \emph{better} or \emph{worse}
when the interval excludes zero, \emph{equivalent} when it lies within a margin of $\pm0.5$ points,
and \emph{no difference found} otherwise. No difference found does not mean equivalent.

\paragraph{Verified runs.} A verified session requires a layout sent at the start; one uncut video
from a fixed camera showing the arena, a code word sent at the start and every run; a trace from
tags or from the converter run by a reviewer; all attempts reported; the agent described (for code,
the routine's version, shared privately on request); and a second reviewer re-scoring at least one
run from raw video. Reviewers are project maintainers or volunteers who have re-scored three
reference runs within tolerance; they declare conflicts and may not review a team, school, family
member or product maker they are connected to. If the two reviewers disagree, a third decides,
and reviewer identities are recorded with the result.

\paragraph{Disputes, versions, minors.} Disputes are re-scored by a reviewer who did not score the
original; every change to a published result is logged with versions and date. Published results
use trajectories, not footage; videos showing people are not published without permission (for
minors, a parent's or guardian's).

\paragraph{Setup.} One VEX AIM robot must sort three orange and three blue barrels into the zone of
their colour on a 1.83\,m\,$\times$\,1.22\,m table within 45\,s (one point per barrel; six maximum).
Four routines, written for the public layout, span common approaches: \emph{dead reckoning} (a fixed
plan, no sensors); \emph{vision} (the camera finds the nearest barrel, wheel odometry returns it to
the zone); \emph{vision + tags} (as vision, then homing on the AprilTag behind each zone); and
\emph{planner} (the barrel positions of the public layout, an efficient order, full speed, the camera
only for the last few centimetres, and spread drop positions). Each routine, each of the six driver
characters and the typical simulated driver play 20 paired runs on the public layout, and 20 paired
runs spread over ten unseen layouts (same start, barrels placed at random away from the zones, the
start and each other).

\paragraph{Results.} Figure~\ref{fig:verdict} and Table~\ref{tab:verdict} summarize the outcome. On
the public layout the planner scores 4.00 points, ahead of the typical simulated driver (2.90) by
a paired difference of $+1.10$ (95\% interval $+1.00$ to $+1.25$), and ahead of every driver
character, including the two strongest ($+0.25$ and $+0.50$). On unseen layouts it scores 2.80: a
drop of $1.20$ points ($-1.60$ to $-0.85$), no detectable difference from the typical simulated
driver ($-0.05$; $-0.65$ to $+0.60$), and below the two strongest characters ($-1.35$ and $-1.00$).
The vision routine scores 2.45 on the public layout and 2.40 on unseen layouts, a change that is
equivalent to zero within the margin ($-0.05$; $-0.50$ to $+0.40$). The dead-reckoning routine,
which also encodes the public layout, drops by 1.05.

\paragraph{Interpretation.} The planner's public-layout advantage comes largely from knowing where the
barrels are. This conclusion rests on a comparison of the routine with itself across layouts and
does not depend on the simulated drivers; the comparisons with drivers additionally depend on the
uncalibrated baseline and should be read as ``outperforms a parameterized simulated-driver
baseline'', not as a statement about real students. Two further observations would plausibly
carry over to real robots: the tag-homing routine loses time searching for the tag, and routines
that drop every barrel at the same spot pick up the barrel already there. At AIM's top speed no
agent sorts all six barrels in 45\,s (best runs score 4 or 5), which suggests the task's budget
should be revisited.

\paragraph{A real robot vacuum.} A household robot vacuum was filmed for 137\,s with a hand-held phone,
without tags; scale came from the robot's size and unseen floor was excluded. It covered 85\% of
3.24\,m$^2$ of visible floor with a 30\,cm cleaning width, driving 18.6\,m. This demonstrates the
pipeline end to end on real footage; without ground truth it does not establish accuracy.

\paragraph{People in an open dataset.} We scored 120 motion-capture walks along one route (about 7\,m)
from THÖR-MAGNI~\cite{thormagni2024}. All 120 reached the goal (median 6.8\,s), so the goal score
could not separate walkers and secondary measures did; with a 60\,s budget a random walk reached
the goal in 40\% of runs, showing that budgets should follow typical completion times.

\paragraph{Index sensitivity.} On the synthetic boards whose simulated agents respect their robots'
limits (two VEX V5 tasks and room coverage), we recomputed the index for 5{,}000 weight vectors
drawn near the defaults (Dirichlet with concentration $50\,w$) and uniformly from the simplex
(Table~\ref{tab:sens}). Under local changes, rankings are stable (median Kendall's $\tau\ge0.94$)
and the top agent almost never changes; under arbitrary weights the coverage ranking is the least
stable. Ranking by goal score alone agrees with the index at $\tau=0.81$ to $0.91$.

\paragraph{Extension: multi-robot matches.} The toolkit also includes a seeded 2-versus-2 match
simulator, a renderer of broadcast-style video from a camera position measured on a real event
broadcast, a per-robot report and a tracking benchmark. A baseline tracker finds 90--97\% of robot
positions with a median error of 7--9\,cm on three simulated matches but switches identities 25--44
times per match (IDF1~\cite{ristani2016} 48--66\%). We present this as infrastructure for future work.

\section{Research roadmap}\label{sec:plan}

Three pilots would test the hypotheses in Table~\ref{tab:status}. Each needs equipment or
participants we do not have and is designed so that a host lab would also obtain a research output.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Real-world capture validation.} The same runs recorded with tags and without, against
    a calibrated reference (for example motion capture), across rooms, lighting and camera
    placements; report median and 90th-percentile error and failure rates.
  \item \textbf{Real drivers versus their own code.} Student drivers and their teams' routines on the
    same robot and layouts, paired by layout, at least ten runs per agent, on public and unseen
    layouts. Involving minors requires parental consent, the host institution's youth-protection
    rules and an ethics/IRB determination.
  \item \textbf{Simulation to reality.} The routines of Section~\ref{sec:experiment} on a real AIM robot
    and table with the same layouts; report the simulator's prediction error per routine, then fit the
    driver baseline to filmed runs and re-check the verdicts.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Limitations}

Most results are simulated and encode our assumptions (robot size, accelerations, camera field of
view, friction, every driver trait). Simulated agents are less variable than real ones --- some score
the same in every run --- so intervals are likely narrower than they will be on real robots. The
synthetic VEX AIM boards published with specification 0.2 contain simulated agents faster than the
AIM robot's top speed; they are marked invalid on the project site and excluded from this paper,
pending regeneration. Tag-free capture needs a visible floor rectangle and has been validated only on
synthetic video. Coverage scores model cleaning rather than measure it. Goal scores depend on how
goals and budgets are set, which is why tasks and protocol change only through public RFCs.

\section{Conclusion}

\mb{} proposes measuring how much of a task's goal any agent achieves, from its trajectory alone,
with an open scorer and an explicit protocol. The framework and tools are built and released; a
preliminary simulated experiment shows why unseen layouts belong in any verdict. Whether the method
measures real robots and real people accurately is the open question, and the roadmap above is how
we intend to answer it. Comments are welcome at \url{https://motionbench.org/paper}.

\section{Reproducing the results}\label{app:repro}
\small
Environment used: Python 3.13, NumPy 2.5, SciPy 1.18, OpenCV 5.0, FFmpeg 6.1, Rust 1.97, motionbench 0.1.0
(specification 0.2); exact versions are written to \texttt{paper/results/environment.txt} on each run.
\begin{verbatim}
git clone https://github.com/motionbenchmark/mb-core && cd mb-core
pip install maturin
(cd python && maturin build --release)
pip install "target/wheels/motionbench-*.whl[tracker]"
bash paper/reproduce.sh     # ~5 min; --full adds the match benchmark
\end{verbatim}
Expected outputs: the synthetic board is byte-identical to \texttt{results/leaderboard-synthetic-v0.1.json};
\texttt{paper/results/aim-sort-verdict.json} reproduces Table~\ref{tab:verdict};
\texttt{index-sensitivity.json} reproduces Table~\ref{tab:sens}; \texttt{capture-accuracy.json} reproduces
Table~\ref{tab:capture} (small differences are possible across OpenCV/FFmpeg versions). SHA-256 checksums
of the deterministic outputs are in \texttt{paper/results/SHA256SUMS} and are checked by the script.

\section{Index definition: edge cases}
$g$ uses the task's maximum score; $e$ is clamped to $[0,1]$; $c=0$ when mean success is zero, and
$c=1-\sigma_S/\mu_S$ clamped to $[0,1]$ otherwise, with $\sigma_S$ the sample standard deviation
($\sigma_S=0$ for a single run, which is why agents with fewer than five runs are unranked); $s=1/(1+\bar v)$
lies in $(0,1]$. All four terms therefore lie in $[0,1]$, and so does the index.
